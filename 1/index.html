Colorizing the Prokudin‑Gorskii Collection

Shu Cao

Between 1909 and 1915, Sergei Prokudin‑Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in color — decades before color film existed — by exposing three black‑and‑white plates through red, green, and blue filters. This project puts those three plates back together automatically, for seventeen of his surviving glass negatives.

01

The problem

Each digitized negative is one tall grayscale image: the blue‑filtered exposure sits on top, green in the middle, red on the bottom. To get a color photo back, I split the scan into its three channels and search for the shift that brings green and red back into register with blue. Nail the alignment, and stacking the channels as R/G/B gives back the original scene in color. Miss it, and every edge in the photo splits into a little rainbow, like this:

How I made that flicker gif

Two frames, saved as one animated .gif:

  1. Build the naive composite: np.dstack([r, g, b]) with zero shift.
  2. Build the aligned composite the normal way, once I know the shifts.
  3. Crop both to the exact same region so the comparison is fair.
  4. In Python/Pillow: frame1.save("out.gif", save_all=True, append_images=[frame2], duration=[900,900], loop=0).

Drop the resulting .gif into media/ and reference it with a normal <img> tag — browsers animate GIFs automatically, no JavaScript needed.

02

Starting small: single‑scale alignment

The three low‑resolution JPEG scans are only a few hundred pixels tall per channel, so before building anything clever I just tried every offset in a ±15 pixel window and kept whichever one scored best. Simple, and it turned out to be enough for these three.

The question I actually spent time on was what counts as a good score. The obvious first idea is L2 distance: subtract the two patches, square, sum — smaller means more similar. It works, but it quietly assumes the two patches should have similar brightness, which isn't really true here: the red, green, and blue exposures of the same glass plate can have pretty different overall brightness and contrast. So I switched to normalized cross‑correlation (NCC): subtract each patch's own mean, divide by its own norm, then compare — that cancels out brightness and contrast differences and compares pattern instead of raw intensity. Larger is better. I used NCC everywhere in the end, and it's noticeably more forgiving on the trickier plates later on.

03

Going big: an image pyramid

The full glass‑plate scans are a different story — about 3200×3600px per channel. The true offset can be over 100 pixels away, and a ±15px brute‑force search doesn't even cover the range, let alone do it quickly on a 12‑megapixel channel.

The fix is a coarse‑to‑fine search. Shrink each channel by half, then half again, a few times over, until the smallest copy is tiny. Find the best offset on that tiny copy first — cheap, since there's almost nothing to search. Then step back down one level at a time: double the offset found so far (since the image just doubled in size too), and only search a small window around that guess to refine it. By the time I'm back at full resolution, I'm only ever checking a handful of candidate shifts, not tens of thousands.

The other change that mattered was scoring on gradients instead of raw pixel brightness. Sky and open water are nearly flat in brightness but look quite different across the red/green/blue exposures — enough that a brightness score can get completely distracted by a huge boring region and ignore the one part of the photo that actually has something to align to. Comparing gradient magnitude instead means flat regions barely vote at all, and the alignment ends up following whatever has real structure — which is usually the subject, not the sky behind it.

04

Wait, why is this so slow?

This is the part I'm actually proudest of, so it gets its own section instead of a footnote.

My first working version of the pyramid did exactly what I described above — conceptually right, but for every single candidate shift, it called np.roll on the entire image and then compared the entire cropped frame. That's fine at the tiny top of the pyramid. It is not fine at the bottom: I ran it on church.tif, walked away, and came back to find it had been running for over nine minutes — for one image, out of fourteen.

Finding the bottleneck

Instead of guessing, I added timestamped log lines around every step — reading the file, building each pyramid level, searching each level — and reran it. The coarse levels finished almost instantly. Nearly all nine minutes were spent in the last, finest level, where the image is back to (almost) full resolution and the search was still rolling and comparing the whole multi‑million‑pixel array for every single candidate offset.

The fix didn't need a smaller search window, just a cheaper way to score each candidate. Instead of shifting the whole image and comparing every pixel, I compare the two images on a strided grid of sample points — say, every 8th pixel in each direction. The shift itself is still an exact integer‑pixel offset; I'm just not spending time reading pixels that a dense score doesn't actually need to make the same decision. That one change took the search from touching tens of millions of pixels per candidate down to a few hundred thousand.

About a 37× speed‑up, and it's the difference between "leave this running overnight" and "run all fourteen images while I go get coffee." I added one more small step after the pyramid finishes: a short full‑precision polish that re‑checks a couple of pixels around the answer on a small central crop, to claw back the little bit of precision the stride gives up.

05

Bells & whistles: automatic border cropping

Every glass plate carries its own physical border — a painted or taped edge, a registration strip, sometimes a hand‑written plate number. Once the three channels are aligned to each other, each channel's border lands in a slightly different place, so instead of a clean edge, the final photo gets a ring of false color where the borders no longer coincide.

My detector checks every row and column of each channel: what fraction of its pixels sit close to that row or column's own median value? A painted border scores very high on this — even where a stamped plate number interrupts it, since the digits only ever cover a small minority of the pixels. A plain variance threshold fails exactly there, because a handful of bright digit strokes is enough to inflate the variance of an otherwise blank strip and make the detector stop short of the real edge. The final crop takes the innermost boundary found across all three channels, so a border only one channel has is still removed.

06

What the harder images taught me

emir.tif

This is the famous hard case for this collection: the sitter's silk robe reflects red, green, and blue light very differently, so it doesn't have a consistent brightness pattern across the three exposures at all. Scoring on gradients rather than raw intensity handled it cleanly with no manual intervention — the result above shows no visible ghosting on the robe, beard, or medals.

harvesters.tif

The foreground row of tea‑pickers and the background field sit at very different distances from the camera, so the small camera movement between the three exposures displaces them by different amounts — a single global translation can't satisfy both at once. An earlier version let the densely‑textured field dominate the gradient score and under‑aligned the people, who are the actual subject. Re‑scoring on gradients and de‑emphasizing the outer border fixed it: the version above aligns the people cleanly, with only the faintest residual softness in the distant field.

masterwoman.tif

This one is worth a closer look. The yurt wall and the patterned carpet she's standing on are sharp — genuinely well aligned, no ghosting anywhere in the background. The color fringing is concentrated entirely on the woman herself, especially her beaded headdress and the folds of her coat.

Because the background lines up perfectly, the camera clearly didn't move between exposures — so the shift has to be coming from the subject. My best guess is that she turned or shifted her weight slightly during the three exposures: a rotation displaces different parts of a body by different amounts and in different directions at once (a shoulder moves one way, a hem another), which a single rigid (row, column) translation has no way to represent. It's a nice, concrete example of the assumption this whole method rests on — a static scene — breaking in a very localized way.
