PART 1

Filters and Edges

Convolution, finite differences, Gaussian smoothing and derivative-of-Gaussian filters on the cameraman image.

1.1 Convolution from Scratch

I implemented 2D convolution with NumPy only, in two versions. Convolution computes \( (I * K)(i,j) = \sum_{m,n} I(i-m,\, j-n)\, K(m,n) \), so the kernel is flipped horizontally and vertically before sliding it over the image; without the flip we would compute cross-correlation, which gives the wrong sign for asymmetric kernels such as \(D_x\). The image is zero-padded so that the output has the same size as the input.

def pad_zero(image, kernel):
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    return np.pad(image, ((kh // 2, (kh - 1) // 2), (kw // 2, (kw - 1) // 2)), mode="constant")


def conv_four_loops(image, kernel):
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    flipped = kernel[::-1, ::-1]
    padded = pad_zero(image, kernel)
    h, w = image.shape
    out = np.zeros((h, w))
    for i in range(h):
        for j in range(w):
            total = 0.0
            for m in range(kh):
                for n in range(kw):
                    total += padded[i + m, j + n] * flipped[m, n]
            out[i, j] = total
    return out


def conv_two_loops(image, kernel):
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    flipped = kernel[::-1, ::-1]
    padded = pad_zero(image, kernel)
    h, w = image.shape
    out = np.zeros((h, w))
    for i in range(h):
        for j in range(w):
            out[i, j] = np.sum(padded[i:i + kh, j:j + kw] * flipped)
    return out
Detail
For even-sized kernels such as \(D_x = [1, -1]\), the padding cannot be symmetric. Padding kw // 2 on the left and (kw - 1) // 2 on the right makes the output align exactly with scipy.signal.convolve2d(mode="same"), which is why the difference for \(D_x\) and \(D_y\) below is exactly zero.

Runtime and correctness

I took a selfie, converted it to grayscale, and downscaled it to 400 × 300 (a 12-megapixel image would take hours with pure Python loops). With a 9 × 9 box filter:

Boundary handling. My implementation only supports zero padding. With a box filter, pixels near the border average in zeros from outside the image, which produces a visible dark frame. SciPy additionally supports symmetric (mirror) and wrap-around boundaries; with boundary="symm" the dark frame disappears, as the second and third panels show.

1.2 Finite Difference Operator

Convolving with \(D_x = [1, -1]\) and \(D_y = [1, -1]^T\) gives the partial derivatives: \(\partial I/\partial x\) responds to vertical edges and \(\partial I/\partial y\) to horizontal ones. The gradient magnitude combines both and measures how fast intensity changes at each pixel, regardless of direction:

\[ \|\nabla I\| = \sqrt{\left(\tfrac{\partial I}{\partial x}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{\partial I}{\partial y}\right)^2} \]

From here on I use SciPy with symmetric boundaries, so the image border is not detected as an edge.

Choosing the threshold

The edge image is the gradient magnitude thresholded to a binary map. Because finite differences amplify high-frequency noise, the grass texture produces many small gradients, so there is a real tradeoff between suppressing noise and keeping edges.

Why 0.25

At 0.10 and 0.15 the lower half is covered in white speckles from the grass, and at 0.20 many remain. At 0.25 the grass noise is essentially gone, while the outline of the cameraman, all three tripod legs and the camera are still continuous. At 0.30 and above, the tripod legs and the bottom of the coat start to break into dashes and the background buildings disappear, so real edges are lost.

The rectangle near the border is not a boundary artifact: symmetric padding cannot create an edge there. It comes from a thin bright frame in the source image itself, which the orientation analysis later confirms.

1.3 Derivative of Gaussian (DoG) Filter

To suppress noise before differentiating, I built a Gaussian with cv2.getGaussianKernel (σ = 1, size 7, i.e. ±3σ), took the outer product of the 1D kernel with itself to get a 7 × 7 2D Gaussian, and tried two approaches:

A · Blur, then differentiate

Blur the image with the Gaussian, then repeat the Part 1.2 pipeline on the blurred image: \((I * G) * D_x\).

B · One DoG filter

Convolution is associative, so \((I * G) * D_x = I * (G * D_x)\). Convolving the Gaussian with \(D_x\) and \(D_y\) (full mode) gives 7 × 8 and 8 × 7 DoG filters, and each is applied once.

The two approaches match: the maximum difference in the image interior is 8.6 × 10⁻¹⁶. Tiny differences appear only in the outermost pixels, because in approach A the second convolution pads the already-blurred image rather than the original.

Comparison with finite differences
With smoothing, a threshold of only 0.09 already removes all grass noise, while all tripod legs and the building outlines survive. The edges are smooth and continuous rather than jagged and broken, but slightly thicker, because the blur spreads each edge over a few pixels. I chose 0.09: at 0.05 and 0.07 some grass remains, and from 0.11 the tripod legs begin to break up.

Bells & Whistles · Gradient Orientation in HSV

The edge images above keep only gradient magnitude. The gradient \((\partial I/\partial x,\ \partial I/\partial y)\) also has a direction: it points toward the fastest increase in brightness and is perpendicular to the edge. I computed this orientation for every pixel (using the DoG gradients, since unsmoothed orientations are dominated by noise) and visualized it in HSV.

Computing the angle without built-in angle functions

I wrote my own two-argument arctangent using only arithmetic and square roots:

1 · Reduce to [0, 1]. Let \(z = \min(|g_x|, |g_y|)\,/\,\max(|g_x|, |g_y|)\), so \(0 \le z \le 1\) and \(\arctan z \in [0, \pi/4]\).

2 · Shrink with the half-angle identity. \(\arctan z = 2 \arctan\!\left(\dfrac{z}{1 + \sqrt{1 + z^2}}\right)\). Applying it twice brings \(z\) below \(\tan(\pi/16) \approx 0.2\).

3 · Taylor series. \(\arctan z = z - z^3/3 + z^5/5 - \dots\) converges very fast for such small \(z\); 8 terms are enough. Multiply by \(2^2\) to undo step 2.

4 · Rebuild the full angle. If \(|g_y| > |g_x|\) use \(\pi/2 - a\); if \(g_x < 0\) use \(\pi - a\); if \(g_y < 0\) negate. This covers all four quadrants and gives \(\theta \in (-\pi, \pi]\).

I compared the result with np.arctan2 only as a check (not used in the computation): the maximum difference is on the order of 10⁻¹³ radians.

Mapping to color

Hue is a circle, just like angle, so it is the natural channel for orientation. I set H = orientation, S = 1, and V = gradient magnitude. Using magnitude as value automatically darkens flat regions, where orientation is meaningless noise. The soft version divides magnitude by its 99th percentile (so weak edges stay visible); the edge-only version uses the binary map from Part 1.3.

Sanity checks
The top of the cameraman's head is yellow-green (gradient points up) because the bright sky lies above his dark hair. In the directed version, the two sides of each tripod leg have opposite colors, since one side goes dark-to-bright and the other bright-to-dark. In the undirected version, both sides share one color (red for near-vertical legs, magenta and yellow for the diagonals), which shows edge orientation independent of contrast. The frame is red on the left, cyan on the right, yellow-green on top and purple on the bottom: every gradient points outward, confirming the thin bright border in the source image seen in Parts 1.2 and 1.3.
PART 2.1

Image Sharpening

Adding back high frequencies, and why sharpening cannot undo a blur.

How it works

A Gaussian is a low-pass filter, so subtracting the blurred image from the original leaves only the high frequencies (edges and fine texture). Sharpening adds these back with a strength α:

\[ \text{high} = I - I * G, \qquad \text{sharpened} = I + \alpha\,(I - I * G) \]

Because convolution is linear, this collapses into a single unsharp mask filter, where \(e\) is the unit impulse (a kernel with a single 1 in the center, so \(I * e = I\)):

\[ \text{sharpened} = I * \big((1 + \alpha)\,e - \alpha\,G\big) \]

I used σ = 2, filtered each color channel separately, and clipped the result to [0, 1]. The single-filter result matches the two-step result with a maximum difference of 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵.

Varying the sharpening amount

At α = 0.5 and 1 the dome texture and carvings become clearer while the image still looks natural. At α = 2 it is very crisp but faint light halos appear around the cypress trees and the dome. At α = 4 the halos are obvious and the JPEG noise in the sky and grass is strongly amplified. I chose α = 1 as the best balance.

Evaluation: blur, then sharpen

To test whether sharpening can restore a blurred image, I took a sharp photo of a church facade with detailed mosaics, blurred it with the same Gaussian, and sharpened the result with α = 2.

Observation
The windows, arches and mosaic outlines become clearly crisper, and the mean absolute error to the original drops from 0.0295 to 0.0248 (about 16%). But the finest details, such as the faces and folds of the figures in the mosaic, do not come back, and mild halos appear along the roofline. Sharpening can only amplify frequencies that are still present; it cannot restore frequencies the blur removed.
PART 2.2

Hybrid Images

One image up close, another from far away.

Method

Following Oliva et al., a hybrid image takes the low frequencies of one image and the high frequencies of another. Up close we see the high frequencies; from afar (or when the image is small) only the low frequencies remain visible.

\[ H = G_{\sigma_1} * I_{\text{far}} \;+\; k \cdot \big(I_{\text{near}} - G_{\sigma_2} * I_{\text{near}}\big) \]

The low-pass filter is a Gaussian and the high-pass filter is the impulse minus a Gaussian. A Gaussian with spatial standard deviation σ has a frequency standard deviation of about \(1/(2\pi\sigma)\) cycles per pixel, so a larger σ means a lower cutoff. A larger σ₁ keeps the far image from showing through up close; a smaller σ₂ keeps only the finest detail of the near image, which vanishes from afar. I added a gain \(k\) to balance the two components.

Pipeline

Align with a similarity transform (scale, rotation, translation) computed from two corresponding points, e.g. the two eyes → crop both images to a region around the subject that is fully covered by the warped image → filter → add → check the result at 1, 1/2, 1/4 and 1/8 size, which simulates stepping back without leaving the desk.

Derek + Nutmeg

How I got here
My first 3 × 3 grid (σ₁ ∈ {4, 6, 8}, σ₂ ∈ {2, 4, 6}) looked wrong in every cell: with σ₂ = 2 only the whiskers survived, with σ₁ = 4 Derek's smile competed with the cat up close, and the best cell sat on the edge of the grid, so I extended the search to larger σ. I also stopped judging from small thumbnails and displayed each candidate at several sizes. Finally, cropping both images tightly around the face removed distracting clothing and background, which made the face-to-face correspondence much stronger. With the tighter crop the cat dominated even at medium distances, so I lowered the gain to 0.55.
Bug found and fixed
An early result had a strong diagonal line in the corner. It came from the alignment: warping the cat image filled the area outside it with black, and the jump from black to image content is a strong edge that survives the high-pass filter. Filling with the nearest edge pixels, and later cropping to the region fully covered by the warped image, removed it.

Favorite: Young Man / Old Woman (full process)

Why this pair works

Aging is expressed mostly in high frequencies: wrinkles, skin texture and thin gray hair. The overall face shape and lighting are low frequencies, and the two faces share them closely after alignment. So up close we see the wrinkles of the old woman, and at a quarter of the size only the smooth, young face remains.

Choosing the cutoff frequencies

Small σ₁ leaves the young man's eyes and mouth sharp enough to compete up close; large σ₁ blurs him so much that he appears only at very small sizes. σ₂ = 8 keeps enough of the woman's wrinkles to dominate up close without her contours surviving at small sizes. I also lowered the gain to 0.8 so that the young man takes over sooner as the image shrinks. With σ₁ = 14, σ₂ = 8, k = 0.8, the switch happens between 1/2 and 1/4 size.

Frequency analysis

Reading the spectra
The low-pass image keeps energy only near the center (low frequencies) and is black outside. The high-pass image is the opposite: its center has a dark hole where the DC and lowest frequencies were removed. The hybrid combines both: a bright center from the young man and the textured outer region from the old woman. I display all five with the same gray scale; with independent scaling, the low-pass spectrum looks black and the high-pass one white, which hides this comparison. The bright cross through every spectrum comes from the discontinuity between opposite image borders, since the FFT treats the image as periodic.

Tiger / Cat

Both animals have striped fur and look alike in grayscale, so this pair was delicate: with the cat as the far image, the tiger's stripes overwhelmed it at every size. The tiger works as the far image because its bold black-and-white facial markings survive blurring, while the cat's large eyes, heart-shaped nose and whiskers carry its identity in the high frequencies. I also found that cropping had to stay inside the region covered by the warped cat; otherwise the stretched border rows turned into vertical streaks.

Motorcycle / Bicycle · a partial failure

The switch works, but the motorcycle's large dark body still shows through up close. The two objects have very different frequency content: the bicycle is almost entirely thin lines with little low-frequency mass, while the motorcycle is dominated by large solid regions. Their spectra do not complement each other as neatly as two faces, which is why I count this as a partial failure.

Bells & Whistles · Color
I tested all four combinations of grayscale and color for the favorite hybrid, with the same parameters.

Color works best in the low-frequency component. Coloring only the high-pass image changes almost nothing: the result looks nearly gray, with just a hint of blue in the eyes. This is because skin tone and hair color are large smooth regions, so the color information lives almost entirely in low frequencies, and the high-pass filter removes it. Coloring the low-pass image gives natural skin tones and makes the far image (the young man) appear sooner as the image shrinks. Coloring both looks almost identical to coloring only the low-pass image. So if only one component gets color, it should be the low-frequency one.

Other explorations in this part
Gain k: balancing the two components directly is often easier than moving both cutoffs; it made the Derek, tiger and bicycle hybrids work. Multiscale viewer: judging hybrids at several sizes at once replaced walking back and forth from the screen. Version-proof alignment: a two-point similarity transform can be computed in closed form (scale = ratio of distances, rotation = difference of angles), so I did not depend on a deprecated library function.
PART 2.3

Gaussian and Laplacian Stacks

Splitting an image into frequency bands, and recreating Figure 3.42.

Stacks

A stack is like a pyramid without downsampling: every level keeps the full image size, so levels can be compared and added directly. Level 0 of the Gaussian stack is the original; level \(i \ge 1\) is the original blurred with \(\sigma_i = 2 \cdot 2^{\,i-1}\) (2, 4, 8, 16, 32). Doubling σ at each level mirrors halving the resolution in a pyramid. The Laplacian stack takes differences of neighboring levels, with the last Gaussian level appended:

\[ L_i = G_i - G_{i+1}, \qquad L_{N} = G_{N}, \qquad \sum_{i} L_i = G_0 = I \]

Each Laplacian level is a band-pass image. Because the sum telescopes, adding all levels reconstructs the original exactly, up to floating-point error; my script verifies this. Laplacian levels are min–max normalized for display, so gray means zero.

Recreating Figure 3.42

I built a Gaussian stack of a step mask \(M\) (1 on the left half, 0 on the right) and blended each band separately:

\[ LS_i = GM_i \cdot LA_i + (1 - GM_i) \cdot LB_i, \qquad \text{result} = \sum_i LS_i \]
Why it works
At level 0 the mask is nearly a hard edge, so a faint vertical seam is visible there, but fine texture stays crisp and is not blurred across the boundary. At level 4 the mask is heavily blurred, so color and brightness change gradually over a wide region. Summing the bands gives a transition that is wide for coarse structure and narrow for fine detail.

Multiresolution Blending

Seamless composites, including an irregular mask.

The method is exactly the one from Figure 3.42, generalized to any mask. All blends are in color, processing each channel separately. My code supports vertical, horizontal, elliptical and wavy masks, and masks loaded from a black-and-white image. When one image must be aligned to the other, I warp it with the same two-point similarity transform as in Part 2.2 and crop all three (both images and the mask) to the region the warped image fully covers.

Bells & Whistles · How many levels?
Blending in color worked directly, and the real question turned out to be how wide the low-frequency transition should be.

With 3 levels (σ up to 4) the transition is too narrow, and the change from grass to snow looks like a hard cut. With 7 levels (σ up to 64) the coarsest band is so wide that the green of the leaves bleeds into the sky between the bare branches, creating a visible green haze. 5 levels (σ up to 16) avoids both. The desert-ocean blend, by contrast, needed 7 levels, because a wide transition is exactly what hides a seam between two similar skies. More levels help when the two backgrounds are similar, but cause color bleeding when the foregrounds differ strongly in color, so the number of levels should be chosen per image rather than fixed.

Other explorations in this part
Choosing images for blending: pairs with the same composition but different content (the same tree in two seasons; two horizons at sunset) blend far better than unrelated images, because the structure on both sides of the seam agrees. Valid-region cropping: after warping, stretched border pixels would otherwise appear as streaks, so the images and mask are cropped together to the fully covered region.
TAKEAWAY

What I Learned

The most important thing from this project.

An image is a stack of frequencies, and which ones we see depends on scale.

Almost everything in this project came from one Gaussian filter used in different ways: subtracting it gives edges and detail, adding that detail back sharpens, combining the low frequencies of one image with the high frequencies of another creates a hybrid, and splitting images into bands lets them blend without a seam. Once I started thinking of an image as a sum of frequency bands, each task became a question of which bands to keep, remove or mix, and how wide the transition between them should be.

The second lesson was that the parameters are perceptual, not just mathematical. The same σ behaves differently on a 300-pixel apple and a 1254-pixel portrait; the right threshold depends on how noisy the texture is; and the right number of blending levels depends on whether the colors on each side of the seam are similar. Every good result here came from looking at a range of settings side by side, at the scale a viewer would actually see them, and then explaining why one worked.
